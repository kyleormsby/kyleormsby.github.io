what is this?

Two ropes are held at their ends by four dancers. Twist: the two dancers on the right swap places, the one walking down passing in front. Rotate: everyone moves a quarter turn clockwise.

Conway’s theorem: the tangle is determined by a single number in ℚ ∪ {∞}. The ropes start parallel at 0, twist sends x to x + 1, and rotate sends x to −1/x. The ropes are drawn as a reduced diagram in a square box, in the style of David Austin’s AMS Feature Column “Untangling Your Square Dance”: tight braids of twists, all turning the same way, with as many crossings as the terms of the continued fraction add up to. Nothing is ever redrawn: a twist adds or undoes a crossing on the right, and a rotation turns the whole box.

On the right is the Farey tessellation of the hyperbolic disk. Twist is the symmetry that slides everything around ∞; rotate is the half turn about the center. The heavy path runs along Farey edges from ∞ through the convergents of the continued fraction to the current tangle. Twisting never changes its length; untangling means shortening it to nothing.

To untangle: if the number is positive, rotate; if it is negative, twist. That is Euclid’s algorithm.
