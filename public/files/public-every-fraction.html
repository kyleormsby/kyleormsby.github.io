How to write all the rational numbers in a single list.

A one-line rule that turns each fraction into the next one.

Part 1

What is a fraction?

Two whole numbers, one on top of the other.

Fractions are for sharing

3 pizzas, 4 people

Everyone gets 34 of a pizza.

ab means: cut into b equal pieces, take a of them.

Mathematicians call these the rational numbers, as in ratio.

Today: positive fractions. Negatives come along for free later.

A fraction is a number; a number has many names

One number, many names

12 = 24 = 36 = 50100 = …

Lowest terms: the name where top and bottom share no factor. Every fraction has exactly one.

“Every fraction once” means every lowest-terms name once.

Where fractions live

Zoom in: they never thin out

Between any two fractions there is another. No fraction has a “next” one.

A worry

So how many fractions are there?

Whole numbers come one after another, with gaps: 1, 2, 3, 4, …

Fractions crowd into every gap, infinitely many at a time.

Surely there are more fractions than whole numbers?

Sizes of infinity

How do you compare two collections you can never finish counting?

Matching

Counting without numbers

Are there as many chairs as children?

Don't count. Tell everyone to sit down.

Nobody standing and no chair empty: same size.

Matching works even when you can't count that high. Georg Cantor's idea: use it for infinite collections too.

Galileo, 1638

A part as big as the whole

David Hilbert's thought experiment · infinitely many rooms, all full

Hilbert's hotel

The key word

Listable

A collection is listable if you can write it as a list (a first, a second, a third, …) so that everybody shows up eventually.

Even numbers: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, …

All integers: 0, 1, −1, 2, −2, 3, −3, …

Listable means the same size as 1, 2, 3, … A list is a matching with the counting numbers. The official word is countable.

Part 4

A tree of fractions

Neil Calkin and Herbert Wilf, “Recounting the rationals,” 2000

The rule

Every fraction has two children

Left child: add the top to the bottom.

Right child: add the bottom to the top.

Start with 11 and keep going.

Calkin–Wilf tree

Grow the tree

Run the rule backwards

Why everyone appears exactly once

Left children are less than 1, right children are more than 1. So every fraction knows its parent: subtract the smaller number from the larger.

The numbers keep shrinking, so the climb always ends at 11: everyone is in the tree.

There is only one way up: nobody is in the tree twice.

This is Euclid's algorithm, about 2,300 years old.

From tree to list

Read it row by row

Look closely: each bottom is the next top.

Moshe Newman's rule

The rule for “next”

1 · SplitWrite the fraction as a whole number plus a leftover.
2 · TradeTake the whole number, add 1, subtract the leftover.
3 · FlipTurn the answer upside down.

32 = 1 + 12 → 1 + 1 − 12 = 32 → flip: 23

23 = 0 + 23 → 0 + 1 − 23 = 13 → flip: 31

next(x) = 1 / (⌊x⌋ + 1 − {x})  ·  no tree needed, no memory of where you have been

Start at 1 and keep applying the rule

Newman's walk

Left = 0, right = 1

Find anyone's place in line

Walk from the top down to your fraction. Write 1 to start, then 0 for every left turn and 1 for every right turn. Read the result in binary.

The mediant

The wrong way to add fractions

12 23 = 1 + 22 + 3 = 35

Your teacher will mark it wrong. A baseball scorer won't: 1 hit in 2 tries, then 2 hits in 3 tries, makes 3 hits in 5 tries.

It always lands in between:  12 < 35 < 23. Its proper name is the mediant.

Lester Ford, 1938

Give every fraction a bubble

The bubble over ab sits on the number line and is 1b × b wide. Bubbles never overlap, and the bubble that fits the gap between two kissing bubbles belongs to their wrong sum.

Cantor, 1891 · one last surprise

Is everything listable?

Suppose someone lists all the points of the number line, as decimals.

Change the 1st digit of the 1st number, the 2nd digit of the 2nd, the 3rd of the 3rd, …

The new number disagrees with everyone on the list. It was left out.

The number line is not listable. Some infinities really are bigger.

One sequence runs the whole list

1, 1, 2, 1, 3, 2, 3, 1, 4, 3, 5, 2, 5, 3, 4, 1, …

Each fraction in the list is one term over the next.

The term in position n counts the ways to pay n with coins worth 1, 2, 4, 8, … using each coin at most twice.

Pay 4:  4  or  2 + 2  or  2 + 1 + 1  — three ways.

Positions start at 0: 1, 1, 2, 1, 3, …
