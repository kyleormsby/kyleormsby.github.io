What's going on

Fix A, B with |AB| = c. The points C with ∠ACB = α form two circular arcs from A to B, mirror images in the line AB. This is the inscribed angle theorem and its converse: on a circle through A and B with centre O, every point of one arc sees AB at half the central angle ∠AOB = 2α, and every point of the other arc sees it at π − α.

The radius is the law of sines, c / sin α = 2R. For α < π/2 the arcs are major arcs (outline of a union of two discs); at π/2 they close into Thales' circle; for α > π/2 they are minor arcs bounding a lens. The shaded region is where the angle exceeds α.

In complex terms the signed angle at z is arg((z − a)/(z − b)), so each arc is the preimage of a ray from 0 under a Möbius map sending A ↦ 0, B ↦ ∞. The preimages of circles about 0 are the Apollonian circles |z − a| / |z − b| = const; the two orthogonal families are bipolar coordinates.

3D. Stack the pictures with height = angle: the surface is the graph of P ↦ ∠APB = |arg((z − a)/(z − b))|, and the picture for α is its horizontal slice at height α. With A, B = (∓1, 0) and α in radians it is x² + y² − 2|y| cot α = 1. Every slice passes through A and B, so the surface contains the two vertical poles and winds around each like half a turn of a helicoid; the two sheets meet in a ridge at π over the segment and fall to 0 along the rest of the line. The blue curve is the slice at π − α: with the locus it makes the two full circles, each tilted across the two heights. "Every π/12" and "Apollonian circles" draw the bipolar coordinate grid on the surface.
