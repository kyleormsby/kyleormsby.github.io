## The short version

You may not submit prose you did not write. You may not submit mathematics you cannot verify. You may use a computer to do computation you understand and could check. When in doubt, ask me — that conversation is never held against you.

That paragraph is also on the syllabus, and if you remember nothing else from this page, it is enough. The rest explains where it comes from, because a rule you understand is easier to follow than one you memorize — and because the reasoning happens to run straight through the subject matter of this course.

## Why the prose rule

In mathematics, writing is not the packaging that thought comes wrapped in. It *is* the thought. You discover the gap in your argument at the sentence where you find you cannot write it down, and that discovery is most of what a proof-based course teaches. A language model will write that sentence for you smoothly and without noticing the gap, and you will not notice it either. Outsourcing the prose outsources the error detection, which is the part you came here to learn.

So: **every word you submit in this course is yours.** Not lightly edited, not paraphrased from something a model produced — yours, written by you, from your own understanding. This applies to problem sets, lab writeups, the project paper, and your talk.

## The principle behind the rest

This course spends its last three weeks on machines that do mathematics. Zeilberger's algorithm will prove a hypergeometric identity you did not find. `ore_algebra` will guess a recurrence you did not guess and then certify it. This is not a loophole in the policy; it is the subject matter, and I want you to use these tools.

What makes them different from asking a chatbot for an argument is **certification**. Zeilberger's algorithm returns a certificate: its correctness is a theorem, the output is mechanically checkable, and you can confirm it without redoing the work. A language model returns a plausible-looking argument with no certificate at all. You cannot confirm it without redoing the work — and if you redo the work, it saved you nothing.

That gives the rule that governs everything not covered above:

> **You may delegate any step whose output you can verify by means independent of whatever produced it. You may not delegate a step you could only check by doing it yourself.**

The second kind is not delegation. It is substitution, and the thing being substituted for is you.

A worked example. Suppose Sage hands you the eigenvalues of a huge matrix. Can you verify them without a second computer algebra system? Yes — because verifying is a different act than recomputing. For a claimed pair $(\lambda, v)$, the product $Av - \lambda v$ is cheap, and the eigenvector *is* the certificate for its eigenvalue. The claimed spectrum must sum to the trace, which you read off the diagonal. And in this course the matrix came from somewhere: $\sum_i \lambda_i^n$ must match a count of closed walks you can do by hand for small $n$, a check that never touches linear algebra at all. Note what the residual does *not* certify — that your list is complete — and that the trace identity is what covers the gap. Different claims need different certificates, and knowing which certifies what is precisely the understanding this policy protects. Running the same computation again in a second CAS is the weakest check on this list: same kind of computation, correlated failures, nothing learned.


## Problem sets

**No AI assistance of any kind on problem sets.** Not for hints, not for checking, not for getting unstuck.

This is not because it would be unfair to your classmates. It is because problem sets are the only place in this course where the difficulty is the entire point. You are not being asked for the answer to Stanley 4.7; I already know it. You are being asked to spend two hours becoming a person who can do problems like Stanley 4.7, and there is no way to acquire that by watching something else do it. A machine lifting the weights for you is not a workout.

Collaboration with each other, on the other hand, is encouraged and always has been — talk to each other, work at the board together, then go away and write your solutions alone and in your own words.

If you are stuck, come to office hours. That is what they are for, and with seven of you I have plenty of time.

## Labs and code

Here I am more permissive, with one distinction.

**The mathematics of a lab is the specification: what you are counting, how you decompose it, what the output should look like, and whether it is right. The implementation is syntax.** You may use AI freely for the syntax — how to get Sage to do partial fractions, why your recursion overflows, how to make the plot readable. You should not use it to decide what the program ought to do.

I will be honest that this line is genuinely blurry at the margin, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. "Help me write the plotting code" and "help me set up the transfer matrix" differ in kind, but there is a continuous path between them. Two things keep it from mattering much:

- **Predict before you run.** Each lab asks you to write down what you expect the output to look like before you execute anything. You cannot predict what you do not understand, and this is also just how one does computational mathematics.
- **You must be able to explain your code line by line.** The standard is exactly the standard for prose: if you cannot explain it, it is not yours, no matter who typed it. I may ask you to do this at the board.

## The project

Everything above applies, plus two requirements specific to the paper:

- **A computation log.** Submit the actual record of what you did, dead ends included. I am not looking for polish; I am looking for the real thing.
- **A paragraph on what did not work.** Every project paper contains a short section describing an approach you tried that failed and why. This is a requirement of the assignment, not a confession. Finished mathematics papers are, without exception, lies about the process that produced them, and part of what you should take from this course is an accurate picture of that process.

## If you are not sure

Ask me. Before, not after. Reed's [Honor Principle](https://www.reed.edu/academics/honor-principle.html) asks you to weigh your conduct against its effect on the community rather than to look up whether a rule forbids it, and that means the interesting cases are the ones that are not covered here. I would much rather have a five-minute conversation about a grey area than have you guess. Asking has never once counted against a student in a course of mine, and it will not start now.

## A closing thought

The last meeting of this course is about the edge of mechanization: which counting sequences a machine can handle, which it provably cannot, and the strange territory in between. D-finiteness is not a side topic — it is the theory of exactly that boundary. So the question this policy keeps circling, *what may be delegated to a machine and what must remain yours*, is not an administrative preamble to the course. It is the course. We will come back to it in December with better tools, and I expect the policy to make more sense then than it does now.

One course's policy is one course's policy: what is permitted here may differ from what is permitted in your other courses, so read each syllabus rather than assuming.
